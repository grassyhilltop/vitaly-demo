Vitaly reads your Apple Watch ECG beat by beat to find premature ventricular contractions (PVCs): early, extra heartbeats that start in the heart’s lower chambers. A few are normal. Frequent PVCs, or several in a row, are worth a cardiologist’s look, especially after a heart attack. Vitaly tells you in plain English which you have.

Private. It runs inside this page; your recording never leaves your device. Personal. It learns what your normal heartbeat looks like and judges every beat against it.

Why we built it

Vitaly started after a family member of one of our cofounders had a heart attack. Their Apple Watch records a medical-grade ECG, yet it kept answering “Inconclusive”, when we knew those recordings could show whether a worrying PVC pattern was there. We believe in preventive sensing: the sensors people already wear should catch the patterns worth a doctor’s look early, privately, and say clearly what to do next.

    Our approach · see inside the box
    
  
    Deterministic preventive sensing: a decision model you can see inside
    
  
    Vitaly is a domain expert system. Its core is a deterministic state machine built from the bedside criteria cardiologists use:
  a beat that comes early, is wide, is shaped unlike your usual beat, and is followed by a pause. On its own that sets a predictable, auditable
  floor: a 78.7% PVC score on patients it never saw. A small learned System 1 decision model, fifteen tiny networks, then
  re-judges each beat the way an expert's quick intuition would, and lifts the score to 95.3%. It stays mechanistically interpretable: every call traces back to measurements you can see,
  and the same recording always gives the same answer.
    
  
    Below, a simulated ECG from a published heart model (McSharry et al., 2003) streams in real time. On the left, the electrical wave
  crosses the heart; on the right, the state machine scores each beat in the open. Press Inject PVC to start a beat in the ventricle instead
  of the heart's pacemaker, and watch it arrive early, wide and followed by a pause.

    Tested on a Raspberry Pi
    
  
    Fast enough on a $15 Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W
    
  
    
    
    We ran Vitaly on a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W: a $15 board smaller than a credit card, with 512 MB of memory, a 1 GHz
    processor and no internet connection. It read each 30-second recording in about 28 seconds, about as long as the watch takes to
    record it, and found the same beats and the same number of PVCs as a laptop on all 11 test strips.
    
    
    That is the same code this page runs, on one of the board’s four cores, in under 100 MB of memory. A private
    health box at home that reads recordings without the cloud is practical on hardware this cheap.

    Why trust it
    
  
    Trained on over 2 million heartbeats from 2,161 patients. Tested on 238 it had never seen.
    
  
    Vitaly learned from 2.0 million heartbeats (103k of them PVCs) recorded from 2,161 people in seven public research databases, then was scored once,

    How it works
    
  
    Rules you can read, small networks you can check, AI only where it is labelled
    
  
    Medical software has to be predictable: the same recording must always give the same answer, and a clinician must be able
  to see why. So every decision here is made by fixed rules and small saved networks. A language model is only ever an optional explainer,
  marked as AI, and it never changes the result.
    
  
    
  
    
    
    Rules
    The first pass is the textbook
    Beat detection follows Pan and Tompkins (1985), the basis of most
    heartbeat detectors. The measurements are the bedside criteria for a PVC: a wide beat, early, with a pause after it, shaped unlike your usual beat.
    
    
    Learned
    Tiny networks do the hard cases
    Rules alone score 78.7%. Fifteen networks of 109k
    parameters each, which compare every beat with your usual beat and the rest of the strip, lift it to 95.3%: 4M parameters in all, small enough for a watch.
    
    
    Readout
    Guidelines decide what it means
    The level comes from a fixed table written from cardiology guidelines
    (AHA/ACC/HRS 2017, ESC 2022): a run of three or more, a 10% or 20% share of beats, bigeminy, trigeminy, pairs. Same beats, same words, every time.
    
  
    
  
    We tested a private language model as the explainer: Gemma 4 (26B), running on a laptop in about 1.6 s per answer. Given the fixed-rule result, it kept the rule's level on 58/58 test cases, never used a number that was not in its input and never diagnosed. Left to choose the level on its own, it matched the guideline rules on only 61% of cases, and every miss was milder than the rules (Claude Opus 5.5, choosing alone: 92%). So the rules set the level, and a model only explains it, labelled as AI.
    

    


    
  
    Built to be personal
    
  
    Anomaly detection against your own heart, not an average one
    
  
    
    
    Hearts differ from person to person, so Vitaly already judges every beat against your own usual beat in the same recording. That is why it
    can spot a PVC whose shape would look normal in someone else. Next it will learn your normal beat from earlier recordings, the way a cardiologist
    compares a new ECG with your last one. Our first version helped on training patients but did not yet beat the single-recording model on held-out
    patients, so it stays switched off until it does.

    What it is, and what it isn't
    
  
    A research preview
    
  
      
    
    • Not a medical device and not a diagnosis. It describes one 30-second snapshot; a 24-hour monitor measures how often PVCs happen across a day.
      • 
    
    • Validated on hospital and patch recordings. On Apple Watch data so far: 21 recordings from one volunteer, where it found all 115 PVCs a frontier AI reader had marked, with 18 extra calls. Not yet validated on watch data at scale.
      • 
    
    • It looks only for PVCs. The watch's own classification covers atrial fibrillation.
      • 
    
    • One of the seven training databases (Icentia11k) is licensed for non-commercial use, so a commercial version means retraining on licensed data.
      •
